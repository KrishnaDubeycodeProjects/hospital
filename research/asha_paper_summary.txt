ABSTRACT
In India, Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHA) serve as indispensable connectors between healthcare sys -
tems and underserved communities, ensuring the delivery of essential health services. At the core of their re -
sponsibilities lies the meticulous documentation of patient information in ASHA diaries. ASHA diaries are 
essential tools for documenting and retrieving accurate information, enabling the monitoring of health status, 
tracking of interventions, and supporting data-driven decision-making. However, navigating and extracting 
relevant information from ASHA diaries presents significant challenges for ASHA workers. Therefore, the pri -
mary objective of this research is to assess the usability of the current ASHA diary by identifying the key 
challenges ASHA workers encounter in documenting and retrieving effective data. An empirical study encom -
passing both qualitative and quantitative methods was carried out involving fifty-seven ASHA workers. Quan -
titative data was collected through a modified questionnaire. Qualitative data were gathered via observation, 
informal interviews, and task analysis to better understand the difficulties in using the diary. Descriptive sta -
tistics and factor analyses were conducted to analyze and describe the factors contributing to usability issues. The 
findings reveal various challenges, including poor visual cues (visibility, readability, legibility), difficulty in 
comprehension, inconsistent data presentation, excessive cognitive load, inappropriate resource utilization, etc. 
By identifying these usability challenges, this research provides substantial insights for improving the design of 
ASHA diaries, ultimately enhancing data documentation and retrieval processes for ASHA workers. This research 
makes a meaningful contribution to optimizing healthcare information systems to better meet the needs of ASHA 
workers and improve healthcare delivery in marginalized communities.
1. Introduction
The National Rural Health Mission (NRHM) was launched by the 
Government of India (GoI) in 2005 to strengthen the public healthcare 
system, particularly in rural areas, to achieve the Millennium Develop -
ment Goals (MDGs). The primary objective of NRHM is to provide 
accessible, accountable, affordable, effective, and reliable primary 
healthcare, addressing various health determinants such as maternal 
and childcare, nutrition, sanitation, etc. Under the mission, a network of 
female health volunteers known as “ Accredited Social Health Activists ” 
(ASHA) was introduced for each village across the country to serve as a 
vital link between underserved communities and healthcare systems 
( Das and Dasgupta, 2015 ; Kumar et al., 2013 ; Panda et al., 2019 ; 
Sugandha and Jagannath, 2019 ). Over time, the ASHA program has 
globally become the largest community health worker (CHW) initiative 
(Ministry of External Affairs. Government of India, 2022 ; Tripathy et al., 
2016 ). ASHA workers are selected from the same community or village 
they serve. One ASHA worker, preferably within the age group of 25 – 45 
years, is assigned for every one-thousand population. The minimum 
educational qualification for an ASHA worker is set at the 8th standard, 
but this requirement is relaxed to the 5th standard if a suitable candidate 
is unavailable. The primary responsibilities of ASHA workers include 
health education, escorting patients to hospitals, diagnosing and coun -
selling, community mobilization, providing essential medicines, raising 
community awareness, and conducting health surveys ( Gopalan et al., 
2012 ; Shet et al., 2018 ; Shrivastava et al., 2023 ). National evaluation 
surveys have shown gradual improvements in maternal and child health 
(MCH) following the implementation of the ASHA program ( Kaur et al., 
2022 ; Shilpa et al., 2020 ).
There is a global shortage of health workers (HW), especially in the 
South-East Asia Region ( World Health Organization, 2018 ). As of 2020, 
approximately 9,14,101 ASHA workers were employed, falling short of

of India, 2020 ; Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. Government of 
India, 2021 ). Shihundla et al. (2016) highlighted that CHWs experience 
heavy workloads in healthcare systems due to their positioning at the 
sharp end of the system. According to ASHA operational guidelines, 
their standard working hours were expected to be 16 h per week or 2 – 3 h 
daily ( Agarwal et al., 2019 ). However, the scope of the program has 
expanded over time, including additional tasks, surveys, and pro -
grammes. Currently, the ASHA program includes more than 30 tasks, 
some of which are unrelated to health and may take precedence over 
health-related responsibilities ( Manjunath et al., 2022 ; Shelley et al., 
2018 ; Smith et al., 2014 ; Tripathy et al., 2016 ). Eventually, the growing 
responsibilities have extended the duty limit beyond 8 h per day, leaving 
minimal personal time ( Abdel-All et al., 2019 ). This excessive workload 
has further contributed to significant stress, fatigue, and burnout among 
health workers ( Pulagam and Satyanarayana, 2021 ; Gopalan et al., 
2012 ).
Research indicates that burdening HWs with numerous re -
sponsibilities, leading to role confusion, difficulty in prioritizing tasks, 
and reducing service quality ( Manjunath et al., 2022 ; Shelley et al., 
2018 ; Smith et al., 2014 ). Additionally, an increased workload can result 
in mismanagement of the information, leading to incomplete patient 
records or inaccurate documentation, which may cause the omission of 
critical data ( Mondal and Murhekar, 2018 ; Mutshatshi et al., 2018 ; 
Shihundla et al., 2016 ).
Documentation is essential for tracking the activities and services 
provided by ASHA workers for patient treatment ( Marini ˇc, 2015 ; 
Oztekin et al., 2013 ). It plays a key role in maintaining records of health 
interventions, service delivery, and community engagement, which are 
vital for evaluating the program ’ s effectiveness and functionality 
( Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. Government of India, 2013 ; 
World Health Organization, 2009 ). However, various studies high -
lighted that the exchange of accurate information is challenging, espe -
cially in rural areas where timely communication is essential for 
managing community health conditions ( Aceto et al., 2018 ; Jackson 
et al., 2017 ; Sokey et al., 2018 ). O ’ Mahony et al. (2014) and Verdezoto 
et al. (2021) discussed the challenges HWs face in collecting, managing, 
and reporting quality health data across the country. Globally, there is a 
growing emphasis on improving the quality of health workers ’ docu -
mentation ( Khattak et al., 2016 ; Nakate et al., 2022 ; Okaisu et al., 2016 ; 
World Health Organization, 2009 ). Gencturk et al. (2017) , Kruk et al. 
(2018) , and Mutshatshi et al. (2018) highlighted that maintaining 
detailed records facilitates effective communication within and across 
healthcare systems, ensuring quality care.
The literature underscores the importance of maintaining high- 
quality health records ( Kruk et al., 2018 ; Shihundla et al., 2016 ). 
ASHA workers are responsible for documenting various health records 
and assisting in monitoring the health status of the community through 
the ASHA diary, which is an official paper record (PPR). Muinga et al. 
(2021) noted that PPRs are pivotal in facilitating multidisciplinary team 
care and tracking patient progress. Despite the increasing adoption of 
electronic patient records (EPRs), PPRs remain a dominant medium for 
recording patient care, especially in low- and middle-income countries 
(LMICs). However, the lack of research on how PPR development 
hampers efforts to improve the designs and their quality. It is important 
to note that many existing records are outdated and inefficient, affecting 
patient care. Bist et al. (2023) and Kohli et al. (2015) reported that ASHA 
workers often struggle with understanding the diary, filling out exten -
sive information, and needing sufficient time to complete the ASHA 
diary. These challenges result in errors, inaccuracies, incomplete re -
cords, unnecessary burdens in interpreting information, poor 
decision-making, delays in information delivery, and reduced patient 
care time ( Ash et al., 2004 ; Kim et al., 2017 ; Pool et al., 2024 ). Conse -
quently, these inefficiencies in the documentation process impact the 
performance of ASHA workers and the overall quality of healthcare 
delivery in rural areas.
Thus, based on the literature, the usability of PPRs remains a critical 
factor that influences the efficiency and effectiveness of healthcare de -
livery. Effective usability of PPRs ensures that healthcare providers can 
easily access, record, and retrieve patient information, directly impact -
ing the quality of care. The usability of PPRs is essential for facilitating 
effective communication, reducing errors, saving time, and preparing 
the groundwork for future digital systems ( Emmerich, 2023 ; Muinga 
et al., 2021 ; Pfeuffer et al., 2022 ).
Addressing these issues, the ability to track health information over 
time is invaluable in rural settings, where resources are often limited, 
and formal health infrastructure may be sparse. By maintaining detailed 
records, ASHA workers can identify at-risk individuals early, ensuring 
timely interventions and improving health outcomes. Accurate and up- 
to-date records enable ASHA workers to provide essential information to 
higher-level healthcare providers, such as doctors and nurses, who may 
be based in distant health centers. Thus, usability is crucial not only for 
enhancing the speed, accuracy, and quality of tasks performed by 
workers but also for ensuring the success of the documentation system. 
To the best of our knowledge, the usability of the ASHA diary has not 
been evaluated yet. Therefore, the primary objective of the current 
research is to conduct a comprehensive analysis to assess the usability of 
the ASHA diary by identifying the key challenges ASHA workers face in 
documenting and retrieving information. The results of this study will 
aid in designing more effective and efficient document systems for ASHA 
workers, improving their usability, and helping maintain accurate 
health records within rural health systems.
2. Methods
This research employed an empirical research design to investigate 
the usability challenges ASHA workers face in documenting and 
retrieving rural health information using the ASHA diary. A mixed- 
method approach was used for data collection, with a detailed expla -
nation of participant selection. The data collection methods were 
designed to thoroughly analyze usability issues by integrating relevant 
usability principles.
2.1. Study design
An empirical study was conducted using a mixed-method approach 
with a combination of quantitative and qualitative analysis between 
April and July in Jabalpur, India. The quantitative component was 
analyzed, simultaneously with qualitative analysis, for an in-depth un -
derstanding of the factors affecting ASHA workers. To maintain their 
diary, a data collection tool is provided yearly by the government in the 
months of March and April every year. A set of modified questionnaires 
(kindly refer to Appendix A ) was used to collect data, followed by the 
conduction of interviews, task analysis, and observational study to un -
derstand the pattern of the problem more deeply ( Bist et al., 2023 ; De 
Groot et al., 2022 ; Khairat et al., 2019 ; Koopmann et al., 2023 ; Meena 
et al., 2020 ).
2.2. Participants
The investigation employed the multistage sampling design recom -
mended by the National Health Systems Resource Center (NHSRC), New 
Delhi, to evaluate the ASHA program in India ( Mahajan and Kaur, 
2022 ). The sampling process involved several stages: selecting a district, 
then blocks, and finally villages (see Fig. 1 ). The district was chosen 
purposively sampling technique based on the data from the Ministry of 
Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW), focusing on areas where women 
had fewer than four antenatal care visits and had become mothers be -
tween the ages of 15 – 19 years, with early marriages, i.e., before 18 years 
(referred to as “ NHM-MP ” ). Within the selected district, two under -
performing blocks were identified and included in the study through 
purposive sampling. Four primary health centers (PHCs) were randomly

2.3. Data collection
In quantitative research methods, a modified set of usability ques -
tionnaire was developed, expecting to comprehensively cover all major 
aspects of usability constructs in a detailed and unified manner. Initially, 
the questions incorporated several well-validated usability frameworks 
identified from existing models and standards in the literature ( Bevan, 
1995 ; Brooke, 1996 ; Koopmann et al., 2023 ; Lee and Kozar, 2012 ; Lund, 
2001 ; Murphy et al., 2019 ; Oztekin et al., 2013 ; Seffah et al., 2006 ). 
Subsequently, the constructs were obtained and refined for relevance, 
applicability, and convenience, incorporating overlapping constructs 
from modified questionnaires. To address the diverse usability criteria of 
the ASHA diary, the research adopted 11 relevant usability criteria and 
included 37 specific metric items. A 5-point Likert scale ranging from 
“ Strongly disagree ” to “ Strongly agree ” , with a neutral category of 
“ Neither agree nor disagree ” , was used to gather appropriate responses 
( Brooke, 1996 ; Gopalan et al., 2012 ; WHO, 2019). The questionnaire 
included closed-ended questions to record the socio-demographic 
profile of ASHA workers ( Kohli et al., 2015 ; Panda et al., 2019 ). The 
questionnaire, initially in English and later translated into the local 
language for accurate data collection, was pilot-tested on 10 subjects. 
Based on their feedback, the instrument was finalized with improve -
ments in language structure, clarity, and spelling. A total of 45 questions 
were used to determine the demographic profile and usability factors 
affecting ASHA workers in documenting and retrieving information 
from the ASHA Diary.
In the qualitative research method, a proficient female researcher 
conducted observations, informal interviews, and task analyses to un -
derstand the challenges ASHA workers encountered in documenting and 
retrieving information. Being fluent in the local language helps the 
researcher to collect data effectively by creating an environment where 
they feel comfortable and can discuss their concerns. The observations 
were conducted in real-world settings, such as rural health clinics and 
community centers (SHC and AWC), where ASHA workers typically 
perform their tasks. The observation focused on management burden, 
environmental effects, retrieval difficulties, and cognitive loads. Face-to- 
face informal interviews were conducted at convenient locations, such 
as residences and AWC, with follow-up questions posed based on the 
depth of discussion and participant engagement. The informal interview 
focused on usability concerns and system understanding. Additionally, 
two to three diverse tasks were administered to thoroughly dissect 
challenges such as time completion, navigation difficulties, and execu -
tion delays in documenting and retrieving information. The process 
spanned 70 – 75 min, during which the investigator diligently recorded 
the data using field notes and, where permissible, audio-video re -
cordings to capture detailed interactions and usability issues ( Murphy

2.4. Measurement
The data was collected to examine the usability problem of ASHA 
workers in documenting and retrieving information from the ASHA 
Diary. For evaluation, we defined the assessment areas based on us -
ability principles that overlapped with the research as follows: Visual 
cues (VC) are defined as readability, legibility, and visibility being easily 
understood and conveniently done for routine workflow ( Lee and Kozar, 
2012 ; Oztekin et al., 2013 ; Seffah et al., 2006 ). Content relevance (CR) is 
defined as the extent to which the content is pertinent and up-to-date 
( Lee and Kozar, 2012 ). Comprehensibility/Learnability (CL) is defined 
in the context of ASHA workers, who can understand the intended 
meaning, easily learn the functionality and gain proficiency to complete 
the tasks ( Koopmann et al., 2023 ; Lee and Kozar, 2012 ). Simplicity (SL) 
is defined as the extraneous elements that are eliminated from the user 
interface without significant loss of information ( Lee and Kozar, 2012 ; 
Seffah et al., 2006 ). Consistency (Co) is defined as the degree of uni -
formity among elements of the user interface and whether they offer 
meaningful metaphors to users ( Lee and Kozar, 2012 ; Oztekin et al., 
2013 ; Seffah et al., 2006 ). Findability/Navigability (FN) means whether 
users can move around in the interface efficiently and locate the relevant 
content ( Lee and Kozar, 2012 ; Seffah et al., 2006 ). Information priori -
tization (IP) refers to organizing information based on its relevance and 
frequency of use, ensuring it is easily accessible and appropriately 
positioned ( Murphy et al., 2019 ). Error prevention (EP) is defined as 
anticipating potential mistakes and incorporating features to guide, 
restrict, and inform users in a way that minimizes the chance of errors 
and their impact ( Bevan et al., 2015 ; Koopmann et al., 2023 ; Oztekin 
et al., 2013 ). Minimal memory load (MM) is defined as whether a user is 
required to keep a minimal amount of information in mind to achieve a 
specified task ( Murphy et al., 2019 ; Oztekin et al., 2013 ). Resources 
utilization (RU) is defined as if the dairy can consume appropriate 
amounts and types of resources for a given timeline ( Bevan et al., 2015 ; 
Seffah et al., 2006 ). Portability (Po) is defined as whether the tools can 
be transferred from one environment to another ( Bevan et al., 2015 ).
2.5. Data analysis plan
The main purpose of this research was to explore the dimensions of 
usability that influence the ease and efficiency with which ASHA 
workers complete their diaries. In this study, exploratory factor analysis 
(EFA), a statistical method, was employed to determine the underlying 
constructs from the questionnaire ( Alnaami et al., 2020 ; Sigudla and 
Maritz, 2023 ; Yu and Richardson, 2015 ). Descriptive statistics were also 
analyzed using mean and standard deviation (SD) to understand the 
demographic characteristics of ASHA workers ( Pfeuffer et al., 2022 ; 
Shihundla et al., 2016 ).
The suitability of the data for factor analysis was verified using the 
Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett ’ s Test of Sphericity, con -
firming the adequacy of the sample size and the presence of correlations 
among the variables. Factors were extracted using principal component 
analysis (PCA), with an eigenvalue as the criterion for factor retention. 
To enhance interpretability, varimax rotation was applied, leading to 
the identification of distinct factors representing different usability di -
mensions ( Koopmann et al., 2023 ; Sigudla and Maritz, 2023 ; Yu and 
Richardson, 2015 ; Zhou et al., 2019 ; Taherdoost et al., 2014 ). To ensure 
reliability, internal consistency was tested using Cronbach ’ s alpha 
( Alnaami et al., 2020 ; Shrestha, 2021 ; Sigudla and Maritz, 2023 ; Yu and 
Richardson, 2015 ). The analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statis -
tics, Version 29.
The queries analyzed from questionnaire were integrated with re -
sponses from informal interviews, task analysis, and field observations 
to ensure accuracy and reliability. Qualitative data complemented the 
quantitative findings (see Fig. 2 ), offering deeper insights into the us -
ability challenges of the ASHA diary. The qualitative analysis followed a 
structured manual approach, where data from observations, informal 
interviews, and task analysis were transcribed and coded using a direct 
content analysis method. Codes were cross-compared to eliminate dis -
crepancies and categorized into broader themes, while recurring pat -
terns were analyzed for consistency and external validity, which is 
tabulated in Appendix B ( Das and Dasgupta, 2015 ; Shihundla et al., 
2016 ; Bist et al., 2023 ).

3.2. Sample adequacy and data suitability for factor analysis
A sample size of 57 ASHA workers was selected for this study. The 
size of the sample was a significant issue when conducting EFA. Several 
researchers recommended various guidelines on sample size 
( MacCallum et al., 1999 ; Sigudla and Maritz, 2023 ). For instance, 
Costello and Osborne (2005) suggested that smaller sample sizes can be 
justified in social science studies. Additionally, Appendix A presents the 
overall distribution of responses, including means and SDs.
KMO and Barlett ’ s test of Sphericity was initially conducted to 
examine the adequacy of the sample and the suitability of the data for 
factor analysis. KMO values range from 0 to 1, with values between 0.8 
and 1.0 indicating adequate sampling, 0.7 and 0.79 indicating moderate 
sampling, and 0.6 and 0.69 indicating mediocre sampling. Values below 
0.6 suggested inadequate sampling for factor analysis ( Sigudla and 
Maritz, 2023 ). A KMO correlation value greater than 0.6 was considered 
acceptable for conducting EFA, especially for samples under 100 
( MacCallum et al., 1999 ; Samuels, 2017 ). In this study, the KMO value 
was 0.608, which indicated that items in the dataset were strongly 
correlated with other items in the EFA correlation matrix, making the 
sample adequate for analyzing the EFA output (see Table 2 ). Bartlett ’ s 
test of Sphericity (Bartlett, 1950) provided a chi-square output that must 
be significant to justify the use of factor analysis. The significant results 
indicated that the correlation matrix was not an identity matrix, 
ensuring that the variables were correlated enough for factor analysis. 
The degree of freedom (df) in Bartlett ’ s test represents the number of 
unique correlations being tested. A higher df suggests that more vari -
ables contribute to the correlation structure, making the test more 
robust. A p -value less than 0.05 is considered good, while a p -value less 
than 0.001 is considered best ( Shrestha, 2021 ; Tavakol and Wetzel, 
2020 ). The results presented in Table 2 show that Bartlett ’ s test of 
Sphericity was significant ( χ 2 = 1803.55, df = 666, p < 0.001), con -
firming that the correlations among variables were sufficient to justify 
the use of factor analysis.
3.3. Extraction and retention of constructs: documentation and retrieval
PCA was used to extract the factors, as it is considered the best- 
performing data reduction approach while retaining most of the vari -
ance from the matrix ( Costello and Osborne, 2005 ). For this study, the 
cumulative percent of variance extracted, Kaiser ’ s criteria (eigenvalue 
> 1 rule) (Kaiser, 1960), and the Scree test were the methods employed 
for retention of factors to determine the number of constructs to retain 
for rotation.
The eigenvalues greater than 1 are considered to draw distinctions 
between constructs. Table 3 depicted that these constructs have rotation 
sum of squared loadings accounting 84.66 % of the total variance, well 
above the acceptable threshold of 60 % ( Sigudla and Maritz, 2023 ; 
Taherdoost et al., 2014 ). However, eleven constructs were identified, 
explaining about 85 % of the total variance, consistent with the eigen -
value > 1 rule, ensuring that PCA effectively captured the main usability 
constructs in ASHA diary documentation. The scree plot in Fig. 3 further 
confirmed that these constructs account for most of the total variability 
in the data, while the remaining constructs contributed minimally, 
indicating they are less significant ( Yu and Richardson, 2015 ). There -
fore, selecting these eleven constructs is considered an optimal choice 
for analysis.

3.6. Qualitative findings
The findings highlighted various usability and environmental chal -
lenges ASHA workers faced in documenting and retrieving information. 
Field observations in rural areas revealed significant design flaws in the 
ASHA diary, leading to many diaries being partially or entirely empty as 
workers struggled to record data. However, those who were highly 
dedicated, had fewer household responsibilities, faced minimal social 
disturbances, and experienced no major health issues were more likely 
to complete the diary despite these challenges. The diary ’ s complex and 
detailed format overwhelmed workers, making documentation 
burdensome. Additionally, the diary lacked quick and accurate infor -
mation retrieval features, forcing workers to flip through multiple pages, 
increasing cognitive load and reducing overall efficiency. Environ -
mental factors such as poor sitting postures, inadequate lighting, limited 
storage in the home, frequent travel, work in different weather, and poor 
infrastructure further hindered usability, particularly in remote areas.
Informal interviews highlighted missing key information, repetitive 
tasks, and workers relying on self-made registers instead of the ASHA 
diary. One of the ASHA workers stated: “ I first collect all the information in 
my self-made registers, which is easy to use, then forcibly transfer the in -
formation to the diary because the inspection officers only check the ASHA 
diary . ” Many workers found the system misaligned with their workflow 
and comfort, leading to frustration, errors, and reluctance to complete 
entries. Task analysis revealed prolonged documentation times and 
difficulties in navigating and locating unclear labeling, formatting, and 
cross-reference entries. The lack of structured guidance makes the pro -
cess tedious and prone to errors. Overall, the cumulative findings (see 
Appendix B ) highlight the urgent need for a more user-friendly, well- 
structured, and accessible diary format that improves data documenta -
tion and retrieval.
4. Discussion
The design of the ASHA diary is pivotal for the effective work of 
ASHA workers and for improving communication within health systems, 
particularly in rural settings. This study examined the usability issues in 
the existing paper-based ASHA diaries that ASHA workers use to record 
patients ’ health information in rural areas. It identified key usability 
constructs, redeveloped them to capture broader issues, and validated 
the measurement instruments. This study also qualitatively investigated 
quantitative data to gain an accurate and deeper understanding. 
Comprehensive usability constructs are revealed through a modified 
questionnaire and pattern coding analysis. While there are challenges in 
generalizing the findings of this research, the study successfully 
demonstrated that the identified constructs possess strong psychometric 
properties and account for a significant amount of variance in the ASHA 
diary ’ s effectiveness. As summarized in Table 4 , this research identified 
the usability problems associated with the ASHA diary and those expe -
rienced by ASHA workers. The key constructs influencing effective 
documentation and information retrieval include visual cues, content 
relevance, comprehensibility or learnability, simplicity, consistency, 
findability, information prioritization, error prevention, minimal 
memory load, resource utilization, and portability.
The visual cue construct encompasses visibility, readability, and 
legibility, and consists of five items with high reliability (Cronbach ’ s 
alpha = 0.898, exceeding the 0.8 threshold). Factor loadings (0.79, 0.80, 
0.83, 0.75, and 0.82) surpass the acceptable 0.6 threshold, confirming 
their strong contribution. This factor significantly influences usability, 
explaining 17.29 % of the total variance with an eigenvalue of 6.40. It is 
crucial to ensure the ASHA diary ’ s elements remain visible under 
various conditions, with high contrast and appropriately sized text. 
Readability facilitates text processing, while legibility ensures clear 
character distinction through suitable fonts and adequate spacing, 
aligning with prior research ( Lee and Kozar, 2012 ; Oztekin et al., 2013 ; 
Seffah et al., 2006 ). Qualitative findings further support this, as one of 
the ASHA workers noted: “ I have to wear my spectacles before filling out the 
diary because the text is too small. ”
Content relevance is another key construct, comprising four items. It 
focused on delivering accurate, up-to-date, and task-relevant informa -
tion for community health workers. High reliability (Cronbach ’ s alpha 
= 0.938) and strong factor loadings (0.918, 0.96, 0.879, 0.859) confirm 
its significance, explaining 10.69 % of the variance with an eigenvalue 
of 3.95 ( Lee and Kozar, 2012 ). ASHA workers highlighted usability 
concerns, stating: (ASHA-1): “ There is only space to tick for vaccination 
completion, but no option to write the vaccination date. Since we require the 
information of date, I separately write it in my register. ” ASHA-2 opined, “ I 
have noticed that some column in the diary is never used. ” These insights 
underscore the need for design improvements, ensuring essential in -
formation is efficiently recorded while eliminating redundant or 
underutilized sections.
Comprehensibility (or learnability) accounts for 6.68 % of the vari -
ance (eigenvalue = 2.47) with high reliability (Cronbach ’ s alpha =
0.856) and strong factor loadings (0.924, 0.894, 0.794) across three 
items. The findings highlight that clear instructions and simple language 
enhance understanding, reduce errors, and improve health intervention 
efficiency. Simplicity, another critical construct, explains 15.61 % of the 
variance (eigenvalue = 5.77). It comprises four highly reliable items 
(Cronbach ’ s alpha = 0.930) with strong factor loadings (0.947, 0.943, 
0.932, 0806), indicating that a simple and concise interface minimizes 
unnecessary complexity, providing clear steps and facilitating efficient 
documentation. However, the diary was observed to be overly cluttered 
and text-heavy, potentially hindering usability. Consistency accounts for 
4.42 % variance (eigenvalue = 1.63) with high reliability (Cronbach 
alpha = 0.856) and strong factor loadings (0.878, 0.754, 0.723). These 
findings indicate that maintaining consistency in structure, terminology, 
formatting, and language improves usability by reducing confusion, 
allowing ASHA workers to navigate the diary more easily and efficiently 
( Lee and Kozar, 2012 ).
Findability (or navigation) identified as a crucial usability factor, 
comprising four items (factor loading = 0.816, 0.805, 0.802, 0.779) with 
high reliability (Cronbach ’ s alpha = 0.866). This construct explains 
7.10 % of the variance (eigenvalue of 2.63), highlighting challenges in 
locating required information swiftly, especially in time-sensitive situ -
ations ( Lee and Kozar, 2012 ; Seffah et al., 2006 ). This is consistent with 
qualitative findings where one ASHA worker stated: “ I find the infor -
mation I need by looking at headings from the title page and then searching 
manually, ” confirming that poor indexing hinders usability. Information 
prioritization explains 2.91 % of the variance (eigenvalue = 1.08) with 
high reliability (Cronbach ’ s alpha = 0.852) and strong factor loadings 
(0.896, 0.870) across two items. However, the current information 
arrangement in the diary is inadequate, making data documentation and 
retrieval difficult ( Murphy et al., 2019 ). Task analysis further confirmed 
navigation challenges, as workers struggled to locate relevant informa -
tion efficiently.
Error prevention is a key construct, explaining 7.47 % of the variance 
(eigenvalue = 2.76), exhibits high reliability (Cronbach ’ s alpha =
0.866) and strong factor loadings (0.910, 0.896, 0.823, 0.745). It 
highlights the need for clear instructions, and redundancy is essential to 
minimize errors. Qualitative findings reveal frequent mistakes due to 
complex layouts and repetitive documentation. One ASHA worker 
stated, “ I sometimes accidently move from one section to another without 
realizing it. ” A well-informed design can prevent such errors in health -
care documentation. Additionally, the memory load is another concern, 
as high-effort tasks can cause frustration, impair communication, and 
delay decision-making, especially in stressful or demanding environ -
ments ( Alshehri and Alanazi, 2023 ; Oztekin et al., 2013 ). The construct 
explains 3.96 % of the variance (eigenvalue = 1.46) with high reliability 
(Cronbach ’ s alpha = 0.819) and strong factor loadings (0.861, 0.854, 
0.620) across three items, emphasizing the need for a simplified,

intuitive design to reduce cognitive burden.
Resource utilization, comprising three items, is another critical fac -
tor (Cronbach ’ s alpha = 0.864, factor loadings = 0.903, 0.834, 0.807), 
explaining 4.72 % of the variance (eigenvalue = 1.75). This is consistent 
with qualitative findings where one ASHA worker stated, “ I use self-made 
registers because some columns, like the due-list, fill up within one or two 
months, making the diary unusable. ” This highlights the need for a cost- 
effective, durable, flexible, and easily updatable diary for long-term 
usability and practicality. Additionally, poor lighting conditions nega -
tively affected the documentation. Portability emerged as a significant 
usability construct, containing two items (factor loadings = 0.928, 
0.898) with high reliability (Cronbach ’ s alpha = 0.915). It accounts for 
3.84 % of the variance (eigenvalue 1.42). The ASHA diary ’ s weight, size, 
and bulky design demands greater physical effort, especially as ASHA 
workers travel long distances to visit patients by foot or bicycle ( Seffah 
et al., 2006 ; Gore et al., 2022 ). The impracticality is heightened in 
constrained or crowded environments like small homes or community 
meetings. One worker stated: “ Carrying the diary while cycling to villages is 
difficult, so I sometimes leave it at home and note things down later. ” This 
suggests that usability improvements should consider the diary ’ s size 
and practical use to enhance portability.
Further, most ASHA diaries were found to be partially or completely 
empty, as many workers preferred using self-made registers. Regardless 
of their age, education, or experience level, they struggled with main -
taining the diary due to its complex structure, time-consuming nature, 
and frequent errors. The data entry is challenging, especially when they 
are constantly on the move, visiting households, assisting in childbirth, 
managing immunizations, and responding to emergencies. It was also 
observed that some physically fit workers with fewer household re -
sponsibilities modified their diaries by simplifying entries to better suit 
their needs. This highlights the lack of adaptability in the official diary 
format, prompting workers to develop their own solutions for efficiency.
Poor working conditions at their workplaces, such as SHC and AWC, 
further hinder documentation. In many cases, workers are forced to sit 
on uneven floors and write in dimly lit crowded rooms, making it 
difficult to maintain neat and organized records. Additionally, diaries 
often suffer damage from dust, moisture, and poor storage, complicating 
data retrieval. These challenges impact the accuracy and completeness 
of health records, forcing ASHA workers to rely on memory or informal 
notes, leading to inconsistencies. The absence of a user-friendly, acces -
sible, and durable documentation system remains a significant barrier to 
effective healthcare data management in rural India.
This study presents several practical and policy-relevant implications 
for improving the effectiveness of tools like the ASHA diary. A human- 
centered design approach is essential to enhance usability and ensure 
the diary aligns with the cognitive and operational capacities of ASHA 
workers ( International Organization for Standardization, 2019 ). Trying 
harder will not work; instead, changing the system of care will. As Kruk 
et al. (2018) said, effective health systems in the 21st century must be 
people-centered to deliver high-quality, outcome-driven services. 
Redesigning the ASHA diary to reflect this principle is therefore 
imperative. The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare should adopt 
user-friendly design standards for all health documentation tools, which 
would facilitate easier data entry, enhance information retrieval, and 
support more accurate record-keeping at the community level.
Specific improvements such as simplifying the ASHA diary format, 
using visual cues and color coding, and aligning the structure with daily 
tasks can significantly reduce the documentation burden and improve 
data accuracy. Focused training on diary use and basic data aggregation 
can further minimize cognitive load and errors. Admon et al. (2013)
suggested that if knowledgeable and capable ASHA workers are 
appointed as data assistants of peers, and further supervised by trained 
personnel at the PHC level, it can enhance the accuracy of monthly 
reporting, streamline data processes, and support overburdened and 
uncleared frontline workers. Collectively, these measures can strengthen 
data quality and management while allowing ASHA workers to focus 
more on patient care.
While this study provides valuable insights, it has some limitations. 
The sample size is small and limited to a specific geographic area, which 
may affect the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, a broader 
range of age groups should be covered to identify which groups are more 
influenced by usability problems. The study found very little work done 
to evaluate usability issues in the paper-based documentation process, 
so the criteria used may not be sufficient to identify all problems, 
especially for ASHA workers. However, the evidence generated by this 
research highlights current efforts to improve PPRs in LMICs. Future 
research should consider larger and more diverse samples and usability 
criteria to validate these results more accurately.
5. Conclusions
Improvement in documentation and information retrieval for ASHA 
workers cannot be done without enhancing the usability of the ASHA 
diary. The findings of this study underscore the critical role of a well- 
designed ASHA diary to augment the functionality and effectiveness of 
ASHA workers, particularly in rural healthcare settings. This study 
developed a comprehensive set of questionnaires for improving the us -
ability of paper-based health records, ranging from visual cues and 
content relevance to portability and cognitive load. The rigorous 
methodological approach of the study, including the development and 
validation of robust usability constructs, not only illuminates the com -
plexities of ASHA diary usage but also offers practical solutions to 
mitigate these challenges. The implications are far-reaching. It sug -
gested that a human-centered redesign of the ASHA diary and assis -
tantship could lead to significant improvements in documentation 
accuracy, efficiency, and overall user satisfaction. Such improvements 
are likely to directly impact the quality of healthcare delivery in rural 
areas, enabling ASHA workers to perform their duties more effectively 
and with less cognitive strain and workload. This research urges poli -
cymakers, designers, and healthcare professionals to prioritize usability 
in health documentation tools, ensuring ASHA workers are well- 
equipped to provide optimal care. The study ’ s recommendations 
should guide future innovations, fostering more effective, user-friendly 
systems that support CHWs in LMICs.

Glossary
ASHA (Accredited Social Health Activist): Community health workers in India who bridge 
the gap between healthcare systems and rural populations, providing essential health 
services and documentation
ASHA Diary: An official record-keeping tool used by ASHA workers to document and 
retrieve community health-related data
PPR (Paper Patient Record): Physical documents used for manually recording and man -
aging healthcare information
Usability: The ease and efficiency with which ASHA workers can use the ASHA diary to 
perform documentation and retrieval tasks accurately
Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA): A statistical method is used to identify underlying re -
lationships among observed variables, reducing them into meaningful constructs or 
factors without a predefined structure
